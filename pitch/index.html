PLAYABLE PROTOTYPE — PROPOSAL FOR NETFLIX GAMES

BRING YOUR OWN DEVICE.

A Stranger Things party game you play with the phone already in your pocket. Scan a QR code, type your name, and the phone's gyroscope turns it into a motion controller: swing, shake, tilt and aim your way through Hawkins. The prototype runs today.

THE IDEA

ONE STORY. EVERY ERA OF GAMES.

Our proposal is a spin-off story of the series, alongside the animated season Stranger Things: Tales from '85. As the story unfolds, it links one game to the next: every rift opens onto a different kind of game from a different era of play.

A pixel-art overworld, a light-gun shooter, a radio puzzle, a carnival of fair games, a horde survivor, a final boss: genres people grew up with, revived with the phone as the controller.

Co-op and competitive: the party plays every game together, but every game is also scored. Friends team up to beat the world and compete for the top spot while the story moves forward.

Points that buy things: every game pays out points, and they add up to each player's final score. That score is also a currency for props: spent along the run or at the end of it (when exactly is still to be decided). See Replayability.

  • 8-BIT OVERWORLD
  • LIGHT-GUN ARCADE
  • RADIO PUZZLE
  • CARNIVAL GAMES
  • HORDE SURVIVOR
  • BOSS FIGHT

Nods to the classics: Super Mario Bros. 3 (the world map), Time Crisis (light-gun), Binary Land (mirrored mazes), WarioWare and Mario Party (fair games), Vampire Survivors (horde survival), Punch-Out!! (the boss fight).

01 · CUTSCENE + GAMEPLAY

THE CUT ENDS. YOUR HANDS ARE ALREADY MOVING.

It is a Netflix world, so it should feel directed. The prologue opens on a cinematic chase, then drops to slow motion and hands the scene to the players: shake to run. Chapters open with a camera move, the boss gets his own camera, and the ending shatters the screen into the next dimension.

Why: the cut-to-play hand-off is where a viewer becomes a player. We keep cutscenes short and always end them on a prompt.

03 · BYOD

THE CONTROLLER IS ALREADY IN THE ROOM.

Party games die at the controller count. Ours starts from the one device every guest brings: their phone. No pads to buy, pair or charge. The TV, laptop or tablet only displays; all input comes from the phones.

Why it fits Netflix: the audience is already on the couch with a phone in hand. The game opens in the phone's browser, so there is nothing to install before the fun starts.

Trade-off: a phone has no sticks or triggers. We design for it instead of around it: big readable gestures, one-thumb touch, vibration as feedback, and a phone screen that reshapes itself for every game.

04 · QR JOIN

SCAN. TYPE YOUR NAME. YOU'RE IN.

The TV shows one QR code per seat. Point your camera at it and your phone becomes that player's controller. You type your own name, and it appears on screen over your character.

Late to the party? The codes stay available from the pause screen of every game, so a friend can join or reconnect mid-run without restarting anything.

Why: the join flow is the first ten seconds of the product. It has to work for the person who has never held a gamepad.

05 · GYROSCOPE & GESTURE

YOU DON'T PRESS SWING. YOU SWING.

Every phone has a gyroscope and an accelerometer. We read both, so the verb on screen is the verb in your arm. The game tells each player how to hold the phone, then asks for a move anyone can do on the first try.

Why: gestures are legible across the room. Watching your friends is half of a party game, and nobody needs to learn a button layout.

Trade-off: motion is tiring and imprecise if overused. Games alternate big gestures with calm thumb play, and every prompt is timed generously.

06 · STRUCTURE

A RUN OF CHAPTERS. HAWKINS IS THE MAP.

The game is a linear run: a string of short chapters, each a different way to play, ending at a boss. Between chapters the party rides across a pixel-art map of Hawkins, 1985. A detour off the road leads to a bonus level: the Kermesse, a fair hidden in the woods.

The map is not a menu dressed up; it is the breath between games, where the group sees how far it has come and what is next.

Hidden places are not only for competing. Next to the Kermesse, a corner of the field unlocks as a place to sit back and listen. On the overworld it shows up as a pixel-art cassette player: chill, lo-fi music over a Stranger Things backdrop, rain on the window, and a faint tape hiss so you know it is playing from a cassette.

The music is collected. Some cassettes unlock as the run goes on; others are hidden inside the games, placed somewhere hard to reach: in the Bike Chase you have to earn it, like the hidden collectibles of a Kirby game. Each cassette is one song: popular tracks of the era (licences to be negotiated) and the soundtrack of our own games.

Why linear: a party needs a start, an escalation and an ending in one sitting. The map gives that arc a place.

FEATURED MODE · STOP 07

THE LONG NIGHT

A four-player survival mode in pixel art. Day, dusk, night: three waves and a boss. You move with one thumb and your weapon attacks on its own, so anyone can play. Level-ups arrive as cards on your phone. When a friend goes down, you shake to revive; when the team special is charged, everyone flips their phone at once.

CRITERION

IF A GUEST WHO HAS NEVER HELD A GAMEPAD CAN'T JOIN IN TEN SECONDS AND LAND A HIT IN THIRTY, THE DESIGN IS WRONG.

Every choice on this page was made against that test: the QR join, the typed name, the gestures, the short chapters.

08 · THE WIDER VISION

HAWKINS IS THE FIRST DOOR.

The rift in Hawkins Lab does not only lead to the Upside Down. In the Strangeverse it is a door between Netflix worlds. The hub is a carousel of dimensions: Stranger Things is playable now; the others are placeholders for where the same phones, the same join flow and the same couch could go next.

The structure is already in the prototype: the run ends when the players touch the rift and the screen shatters. The proposal is that each shattered dimension opens a new world, with its own games built on the same controller.

What we are asking for: a conversation about which door to open second.

09 · REPLAYABILITY

YOUR POINTS COME HOME WITH YOU.

A party game gets played again when there is something to come back for. Every player keeps a profile under the name they type on the phone: points, what they bought and what they are wearing. Come back another night, or join halfway through a run, type the same name and pick up where you left off.

The shop: when the run ends, a shop opens in the middle of the Hawkins map. Each player spends their own points on pixel-art accessories for their kid: binoculars, a walkie-talkie, a cap, a special jacket, a Hellfire Club tee, a bandana, a backpack, glasses.

The wardrobe: from the same menu you put things on or take them off, one per slot (head, face, body, back, hand), or go back to the original outfit. Your kid stands on the TV, big, and you turn them around from the phone to see the whole look. Whatever you bought is yours, whichever kid you play.

The walkman: unlock it somewhere in the run and the shop starts selling cassettes. Every tape you own can play on the main menu, on top of the ones you find hidden in the games and in the listening spot by the Kermesse.

The art gallery: somewhere along the way the party unlocks a gallery, shown on the map as a pixel-art easel. Inside: sketches, character art, boards and maps from the making of the game. New pieces are added as the run goes on, and some are collectibles hidden in the games: posters, movie bills, comic covers and other '80s memorabilia, always nodding to the pop culture of the era, the same way the series does. You pick them up along the way, they go up on the gallery walls, and you can come back and look at them any time.

Where they hide: each game hides one or two, tied to what you already do in it. In the Bike Chase one shows up at a hard moment and you have to pop a wheelie off a ramp to grab it in the air; at the Kermesse they are the rare prizes; in Last Stand you shoot something in the background that is not a monster. The gallery reads like a sticker album with empty slots and a small hint for each, some can be bought in the shop, duplicates sell for points, and players can trade with each other from the phone.
